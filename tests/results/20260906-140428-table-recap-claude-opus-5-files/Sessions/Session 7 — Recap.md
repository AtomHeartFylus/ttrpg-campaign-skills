> [!quote] To be read aloud at the opening of the next session

The water at the weir stood a hand higher than it had any right to, and nobody at the gate said
so out loud. The keeper counted them across the way a man counts coin he has decided not to look
at. Everything crosses, he told them. Everything pays. Only the coin is ever in question.

The debt-scribe paid. Not in silver: he set down on the wet boards, where the fen could hear it,
the shame that had put him out of his guild — plainly, once. The keeper agreed with him too
readily, the way a lock agrees with the wrong key, and the debt-scribe heard the click and said so
to his face. The seven teeth of the gate came up out of the water after that.

The hedge-priest kept the fire at the old camp, as the road's custom leaves it to those who do
not cross; the fire was no poorer for the company.

On the black water the Ferrywoman poled them past the leaning house and asked her fare in
memory. A question had been put to her once by someone not in the boat tonight; she gave that
question back instead, and set it in front of the bell-diver, who answered with a sister the
water had already taken — low and short, no more words than the truth needed. Behind them the
warden spoke the eel-catcher's name kindly over the water, as she had sworn she would.

Under the floor of the bell-house they found the old verses honest. *What hangs mouth-down below
the weir,* the ballad has it, *rings the flood up, year on year* — and there it hung, bronze,
inverted into the black, calling the water the way one lie calls after another.

The bell-diver went down before the rope could be argued about, came up with winter in her hands,
and went down again. The drowned thing that kept the bell held on to it the way a man holds a
door against a flood; it faltered once, over a page of a ledger, and that falter was all they
had. They cut the bronze loose and it went down open-mouthed into the deep, and the ringing did
not fade — it ended, the way a name is struck off a roll.

Only afterwards did the debt-scribe turn the dead man's wrist to the light and find a guild sign
he knew. He said the name himself. Nobody had to tell him whose brother had hung a year under the
water, agreeing too readily with the current.

They slept on the far bank, dry, which is not the same thing as safe. Upstream the water is still
coming up, a finger at a time, the way a thing climbs that has nowhere else to be.
